**Instructor feedback on Geon’s draft**

(Consider this evaluation for your revision.)

**Thesis and Research Question B**

The research question whether AI judges can serve as a viable alternative to human judges is timely and intellectually ambitious. 

The thesis is stated clearly in the introduction: AI judge can be the best alternative to human judges, despite its imperfections, 

because it can overcome the inherent limitations of human subjectivity. 

The argument follows a logical arc from legal theory to human limitations to AI as solution. 

However, the introduction does not follow the CARS model as required by the rubric. 

The opening jumps immediately into the paper's argument without adequately 

establishing the territory of existing research on judicial trust, AI in law, or legal philosophy. 

There is no move that situates this paper within a broader scholarly conversation, 

and the niche the gap this paper addresses  is only vaguely implied rather than explicitly identified. 

Restructuring the introduction with all three CARS moves would significantly strengthen this opening. 

Additionally, the thesis would benefit from hedging: claiming AI "can be the best alternative" is an overstatement given the paper's own acknowledgment 

that AI is "not a perfect technology." A more carefully qualified claim would be more defensible and more appropriate for academic writing.

**Complexity and Focus B+**

The paper demonstrates genuine intellectual ambition by working through two competing legal theories before arriving at its argument about AI. 

The decision to use natural law theory and legal positivism as an evaluative framework for AI judging is original 

and conceptually interesting not many undergraduate papers anchor a technology argument in legal philosophy.

The discussion of Hart's core/penumbra distinction and its relevance to algorithmic decision-making shows real analytical thinking.

However, the paper's complexity is undermined by its tendency to resolve tensions too quickly.

Every counterargument  that AI is biased, that algorithms can be misused, 

that integrating legal theories creates problems  is acknowledged and then dismissed in one or two sentences with the claim

that "this problem can be resolved enough as technology advances." This is the central analytical weakness of the draft. 

The section on popular originalism and Balkin's theory is genuinely interesting but arrives too late and too briefly to be fully developed.

**Research B+**
Seven sources are used, meeting the minimum requirement. The range across legal philosophy (Hart, 1958), 

empirical psychology (Irwin & Real, 2011), legal theory journals (Donnelly, 2025; Novak, 2025), 

and AI policy research (Javed & Li, 2025) reflects genuine interdisciplinary thinking. 

Hart (1958) is particularly well chosen as a foundational anchor for the legal theory argument. 

However, source integration has significant problems throughout. 

Most critically, Hart (1958) is extensively paraphrased across an entire section without a single direct quotation or specific page reference, 

making it difficult to verify whether the claims accurately represent Hart's argument or have been filtered through secondary sources. 

The paper also refers to Collins and Komatsu (2025) and Wolf (2024) to support the originalism critique but does not provide specific claims, 

evidence, or direct engagement with what those sources actually argue. 

They are cited as endorsements rather than integrated as voices in a conversation. 

The rubric requires that research be "synthesized and effectively commented on,"

which means the student's own analysis must connect multiple sources rather than citing them in isolation.

The paper also repeats "Other authors question the usefulness of such an approach. 

Other authors (see Collins & Komatsu, 2025; Wolf, 2024) question the usefulness of originalism arguing that..." 

which is an awkward repetition that should be cleaned up.

**Structure and Organization A-**

The four section headings are clearly labeled and the logical sequence — natural law theory, 

legal positivism, human judge problems, AI judge evaluation — is coherent and purposeful. 

The paper earns credit for building its argument systematically rather than jumping directly to its conclusion.

The conclusion is focused and returns to the paper's key claims. However, the conclusion does not fulfill the rubric's requirements adequately. 

It does not acknowledge limitations of the research, does not suggest directions for future study beyond brief mentions in passing, 

and does not discuss the paper's broader social significance in analytical depth. 

Sentence-level coherence also needs work throughout. Many sentences are very long and syntactically complex in ways that make them difficult to parse, 

and the Given–New contract breaks down frequently as new ideas are introduced without adequate grounding from previous sentences.

**Overall Assessment**
This draft tackles one of the most genuinely original research questions submitted this semester. 

The paper shows real engagement with difficult theoretical material and demonstrates that Geon has read and thought seriously 

about Hart, originalism, and the limitations of human judgment. 

The primary revision priorities for the final version are: restructuring the introduction using the CARS model, 

revising the entire draft for sentence clarity and academic register, 

developing counterarguments more fully rather than dismissing them with "technology can solve this," 

adding specific page references and direct quotations from key sources especially Hart, 

consolidating the redundant citation block, strengthening the conclusion with limitations and future directions.
